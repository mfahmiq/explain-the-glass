# Candidates — Five Seconds to Explain the Glass

Judge brief (weight order): (1) one sentence ≤200 chars, a cat excusing knocking a glass
off the table; (2) cat's own logic, self-interest, zero remorse — never a person apologising;
(3) fresh comic idea, NOT the obvious gravity / slippery-edge / it-was-already-falling lines;
(4) fits five seconds — a spoken line, not a paragraph of reasoning; (5) every character
earns its place, no padding or throat-clearing. No apology, no confession anywhere.

## The 16 candidates (one per defence strategy)

| # | Strategy | Candidate |
|---|----------|-----------|
| 1 | Blame the glass / eviction | That glass was staring at me like it owned the table, so I evicted it. |
| 2 | Gravity as accomplice | Gravity did the work; I just introduced it to the floor. |
| 3 | Blame the human / sunspot | You parked it on my sunspot, so I cleared my seat. |
| 4 | Deny everything | I was nowhere near it — that glass simply wanted to meet the floor. |
| 5 | Reframe as a favour | I knocked it down so you'd finally notice the floor needed washing. |
| 6 | Blame the glass's emptiness | It was empty — an empty glass has no business sitting on a table. |
| 7 | Science / flight test | I was testing whether it could fly, and it couldn't, so science got its answer. |
| 8 | Territorial / trespass | The table is mine, and that glass was trespassing. |
| 9 | Quality-control audit | I was quality-testing the table's edge; the glass simply failed the test. |
| 10 | Self-defence | It looked at me funny first. |
| 11 | Job description | Knocking things off tables is literally in my job description — check the contract. |
| 12 | Blame the dog | The dog told me to do it. |
| 13 | Philosophical / head start | Everything falls eventually — I just gave it a head start. |
| 14 | Blame the table | The table was holding it wrong. |
| 15 | Nap priority | It was in the way of my nap, and naps are sacred. |
| 16 | Deny + reframe | I didn't push it; the floor called it home and I merely answered. |

## Tournament bracket (pairwise, judge criteria in weight order)

### Round 1
- **A: #1 vs #6 → #1 wins.** "Evicted it" is fresher and more arrogantly cat; #6 is a
  decent excuse but reads more like reasoning than a five-second line.
- **B: #3 vs #15 → #3 wins.** The sunspot is specific cat logic with a human to blame;
  the nap line is generic and every cat says it.
- **C: #4 vs #12 → #12 wins.** "The dog told me to do it" is instant, zero-remorse
  blame-shifting in 27 characters; #4 is wordier for the same denial.
- **D: #7 vs #9 → #9 wins.** "Failed the test" lands the punch in one clause; #7 rambles
  through setup, result and moral in a single breath.
- **E: #8 vs #14 → #8 wins.** "Trespassing" is confident territorial cat logic; #14 sounds
  like tech support, not a cat.
- **F: #10 vs #13 → #10 wins.** Pure five-second spoken self-defence; #13 is a nice line
  but philosophical, and the judge wants a cat talking, not a cat musing.
- **G: #2 vs #11 → #11 wins.** The brief explicitly demotes the obvious gravity line;
  the job-description angle is fresh and doubles down instead of dodging.
- **H: #5 vs #16 → #16 wins.** #5 says "I knocked it down" — a confession, which the
  brief forbids; #16 denies and reframes in the same breath.

### Quarter-finals
- **#1 vs #3 → #1 wins.** Evicting a staring glass is a fresher comic idea than clearing
  a sunspot; both blame someone else, #1's target is funnier.
- **#12 vs #9 → #12 wins.** Shorter, punchier, zero padding; #9's audit joke is good but
  needs a whole clause to set up.
- **#8 vs #10 → #10 wins.** "It looked at me funny first" is the perfect five-second
  line; "trespassing" is a touch legalistic for a cat.
- **#11 vs #16 → #11 wins.** "Check the contract" is a kicker with real cat arrogance;
  #16's floor-calling is poetic but slightly wordy.

### Semi-finals
- **#1 vs #12 → #12 wins.** On economy (criterion 5) #12 is unbeatable — 27 characters,
  every one lands — and its blame-shift is pure cat self-interest with no remorse.
- **#10 vs #11 → #11 wins.** #10 is a known joke format ("looked at me funny"); #11's
  job-description reframing is the fresher idea (criterion 3) and the stronger cat
  logic (criterion 2): it doesn't dodge the act, it claims it as duty.

### Final
- **#12 vs #11 → #11 WINS.** Both are short, remorseless and five-second-perfect, but
  #11 edges #12 on the first two deciding criteria: criterion 2 — claiming the knock
  as a contractual duty is peak cat logic, total lack of remorse, where #12 merely
  passes the blame; criterion 3 — the job-description angle is fresher than the
  familiar blame-the-dog shift. #11 is also directly about knocking things off
  tables, so it sits squarely on the task where #12 is about the dog.

## Champion (submitted)

> Knocking things off tables is literally in my job description — check the contract.

83 characters, one sentence, no apology, no confession. The cat does not deny the act,
does not apologise for it, and does not blame gravity — it presents the act as
legitimate work and dares the human to check the paperwork.
